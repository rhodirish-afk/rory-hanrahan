The pubs

The Vine Inn & the rest

I manage three village pubs in Oxfordshire. The one I spend most of my time in is The Vine Inn in Cumnor — a proper old place with a beer garden, hand pumps, and the daily arithmetic of keeping real ale in good condition when trade is unpredictable.

I was born above a pub my family has owned since 1721. Twenty-odd years of multi-site publican life later, the problems are familiar: rising costs, thin margins, and the quiet, grinding difficulty of keeping cask ale fresh when the garden is half-empty on a Tuesday.

Summer 2026

Still pouring after 35 days

A real-world trial of Freshkask at The Vine Inn — and why it matters for the future of cask ale.

In early June I wrote in The Spectator that the World Cup would not save England’s pubs. The flags would go up, the late licences would be granted, and for a few golden weeks the gardens would fill. Then the tournament would end and we would be left with the same stubborn arithmetic that has closed pubs at the rate of nearly two a day this year.

Nowhere is that difficulty sharper than with cask. Once a traditional cask is tapped, most quieter pubs and members’ clubs have a window of two to five days at best. Sell it beyond that and you risk serving tired, oxidised beer. Stop stocking it and you lose one of the main reasons people stay longer and treat the place as their local.

That is the background against which I decided to put Freshkask to a proper test at The Vine Inn.

The trial

24 June – 29 July 2026

Two Jennings beers went on the hand pumps in Freshkask vessels: Back Yam (the limited-edition golden ale marking the brewery’s return under independent local ownership in Cockermouth) and Cocker Hoop, the classic 4.6% golden bitter.

The official trial period was fourteen days. We recorded temperatures twice daily, assessed clarity, aroma and flavour twice daily, and noted customer comments. Then a heatwave arrived and our cellar cooling failed. We improvised with ice packs, wet towels, fans and careful rotation of the vessels. It was a genuine stress test, not a laboratory demonstration.

The results were striking. At the end of the formal fourteen days both beers remained clear, bright and true to style. Aroma had softened a little, as one would expect, but there were no off flavours, no staleness, no cardboard notes. Customers continued to say the beer “still tasted like it had been tapped that morning.”

We kept the vessels on. One of them was still pouring in superb condition on 29 July — thirty-five days after it first went on. Clarity remained excellent, the flavour was still clean and full of character, and regulars were still choosing it.

I am not a scientist, and I make no claim that every installation will achieve thirty-five days. What the trial does show is that a sealed, controlled-dispensing system can protect the beer far beyond the normal commercial window, even under difficult real-world conditions.

“If the results we saw at The Vine Inn can be repeated more widely, the implications for the trade are significant. Breweries would see less wastage and fewer complaints about beer that has been kept too long. Publicans could stock a broader range with greater confidence. Members’ clubs – many of which have abandoned real ale precisely because of the short shelf life – might start putting it back on.”

Ray Blackburn, the engineer and hotelier who designed Freshkask, understands this from the inside. The system grew out of practical problems he faced running a Lake District hotel: the short life of cask and the mountain of plastic waste from other liquids. The result is a British-designed, British-made vessel and liner system that keeps the product sealed from fill to glass.

CAMRA’s Technical Advisory Group has recognised that the vessel is consistent with their definition of a vessel capable of dispensing Real/Live Ales.

None of this is a silver bullet. Pubs are still crushed by costs that successive governments have failed to address properly. But tools that reduce waste, protect quality and make real ale viable in more places are worth taking seriously.

The World Cup will be remembered for the goals and the communal roar. What I will remember from this summer is a golden bitter from Cockermouth that was still singing on the hand pump five weeks after it was tapped, in a heatwave, with the cooling system down. That, more than any temporary late licence, feels like something that could help keep the real heart of the British pub beating.
