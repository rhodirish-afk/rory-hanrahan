Still pouring after 35 days

A real-world trial of Freshkask at The Vine Inn — and why it matters for the future of cask ale.

29 July 2026 · By Rory Hanrahan

In early June I wrote in The Spectator that the World Cup would not save England’s pubs. The flags would go up, the late licences would be granted, and for a few golden weeks the gardens would fill. Then the tournament would end, the temporary TVs would come down, and we would be left with the same stubborn arithmetic that has closed pubs at the rate of nearly two a day this year: rising costs, thin margins, and the quiet, grinding difficulty of keeping real ale in good condition when trade is unpredictable.

Nowhere is that difficulty sharper than with cask. Once a traditional cask is tapped, most quieter pubs and members’ clubs have a window of two to five days at best. Sell it beyond that and you risk serving tired, oxidised beer that damages the drinker’s trust and the brewery’s reputation. Stop stocking it and you lose one of the main reasons people stay longer, spend more, and treat the place as their local. For many struggling venues the choice is bleak.

That is the background against which I decided to put Freshkask to a proper test at The Vine Inn in Cumnor.

On 24 June 2026 we put two Jennings beers on the hand pumps in Freshkask vessels: Back Yam, the limited-edition golden ale that marked the brewery’s return under independent local ownership in Cockermouth, and Cocker Hoop, the classic 4.6 per cent golden bitter.

The official trial period was fourteen days. We recorded temperatures twice daily, assessed clarity, aroma and flavour twice daily, and noted customer comments.

The weather, however, had other ideas. Extreme heat arrived and our cellar cooling failed. We improvised with ice packs, wet towels, fans and careful rotation of the vessels into cooler spots. It was a genuine stress test, not a laboratory demonstration.

The results were striking. At the end of the formal fourteen days both beers remained clear, bright and true to style. Aroma had softened a little, as one would expect, but there were no off flavours, no staleness, no cardboard notes.

Customers continued to comment that the beer “still tasted like it had been tapped that morning.” We kept the vessels on. One of them was still pouring in superb condition on 29 July — thirty-five days after it first went on. Clarity remained excellent, the flavour was still clean and full of character, and regulars were still choosing it.

I am not a scientist, and I make no claim that every installation will achieve thirty-five days. What the trial does show is that a sealed, controlled-dispensing system can protect the beer far beyond the normal commercial window, even under difficult real-world conditions.

For a busy city pub that turns over cask quickly this may be interesting rather than essential. For the quieter village pub, the members’ club, or the free house that wants to offer a proper range without constant wastage, it is a game changer.

From a brewing point the savings are immense. No more hours spent cleaning casks with expensive caustic acid and battling with beerstone. Brewers can get on with their real job of innovation — brewing instead of cleaning.

Ray Blackburn, the engineer and hotelier who designed Freshkask, understands this from the inside. Like me, he has a lifelong passion for pubs and real ale. The system grew out of practical problems he faced running a Lake District hotel: the short life of cask and the mountain of plastic waste from other liquids.

The result is a British-designed, British-made vessel and liner system that keeps the product sealed from fill to glass, measures the dispense, and removes the need for repeated caustic cleaning of traditional containers. It is not a gimmick. It is a piece of practical engineering in service of a tradition that many of us care about deeply.

If the results we saw at The Vine Inn can be repeated more widely, the implications for the trade are significant. Breweries would see less wastage and fewer complaints about beer that has been kept too long. Publicans could stock a broader range with greater confidence. Members’ clubs — many of which have abandoned real ale precisely because of the short shelf life — might start putting it back on.

Quieter pubs would no longer face the daily anxiety of whether today’s cask will still be drinkable on Friday. And the image of cask ale itself would be protected from the damage done by tired beer served in desperation.

None of this is a silver bullet. Pubs are still crushed by costs that successive governments have failed to address properly. But tools that reduce waste, protect quality and make real ale viable in more places are worth taking seriously.

I am currently working with The Spectator on a “Save the British Pub” campaign that will run alongside the existing efforts to protect parish churches. Both are about defending the institutions that still hold communities together.

Freshkask will not solve the tax and regulatory burden on its own, but the trial has left me genuinely excited that we may have a practical way to keep better beer in more pubs for longer.

The World Cup will be remembered for the goals, the flags and the communal roar. What I will remember from this summer is a golden bitter from Cockermouth that was still singing on the hand pump five weeks after it was tapped, in a heatwave, with the cooling system down. That, more than any temporary late licence, feels like something that could help keep the real heart of the British pub beating.
