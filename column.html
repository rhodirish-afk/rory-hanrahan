After the Settlement

21 August 2026

Peter McCormack was forty-two when he was murdered. On the evening of 19 November 1992 he was in the Thierafurth Inn in Kilcoo for a charity darts match. UVF gunmen walked in and opened fire. McCormack was killed. Three others were injured. It was a sectarian murder by a loyalist gang that treated Catholic lives as disposable. That fact has never been in doubt.

For more than thirty years the public story stayed simple. A Catholic civilian teacher murdered for being Catholic. In 2016 the Police Ombudsman linked the same UVF unit to the Loughinisland massacre. The High Court later found plausible evidence of significant state collusion. Military intelligence had been used by the killers. There were links to a UDR member. Survivors of the attack were awarded a substantial settlement this year, reported at more than four million pounds. McCormack’s own case was not part of that group payment. The wider finding of collusion stood.

Then the story changed. Last week, at a republican commemorative event in County Down, Peter McCormack was publicly claimed as Óglach — an IRA volunteer — with a formal memorial plaque of the kind reserved for recognised members of the Provisional movement. The civilian teacher became a soldier of the Republic, decades after his death and after the legal and financial processes that treated him as a civilian had largely finished.

This does not make the murder any less evil. The UVF gunmen who opened fire in a pub remain what they were. A sectarian killing is a sectarian killing. The question is what the late change of status does to the record.

If he was an IRA volunteer, that fact was relevant from the start. It does not justify the murder. It alters the context in which the High Court and the Ombudsman examined the events. When one side of the story is held back for decades and released only after settlements have been paid and findings recorded, the process itself is damaged. The timing is not a minor detail. It is the point.

The larger question follows. How many other “civilians” in the legacy files were, in reality, active members of the Provisional IRA? This is not a conspiracy claim. It is a demand for the same standard of truth in every direction. In one direction the state is examined with forensic intensity. In the other, the preferred categories of the republican narrative — civilian, volunteer, non-combatant — are often accepted until it becomes useful to change them.

Legacy processes claim to seek truth. They deliver a usable version of the past. Cases that fit a preferred narrative receive attention and institutional weight. Cases that complicate the narrative stay quieter. When a man can be a civilian for the purposes of court and settlement, and a volunteer for the purposes of commemoration, the system is not delivering truth. It is managing it.

Peter McCormack was murdered in a pub while playing darts. That remains a fact. The men who killed him were sectarian killers. That remains a fact. The late claim that he was an IRA man does not erase either. It does require that the official record, and the public memory built on it, be treated with more caution than they have so far received.

The settlement has been paid. The findings have been recorded. The status has now changed. The question that follows is simple: what else has been left out?

It’s hot. Stop telling me.

11 August 2026

It’s hot. There is really no way of denying this, but I swear to God that the next person who feels the need to tell me that it’s hot, I will punch them…
