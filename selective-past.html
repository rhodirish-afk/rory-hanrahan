Long Form

The Selective Past

20 August 2026 · ~2,400 words

I keep coming back to the numbers.

Nuremberg took less than a year for the main trial. The rest was done by 1949. Four years, start to finish. South Africa’s Truth Commission did the bulk of its work in six or seven years. Both had a beginning. Both had an end. Both stopped.

The costs tell the same story. Nuremberg cost the British side low hundreds of thousands of pounds at the time. A rounding error now. The South African commission ran on a clear budget and then stopped. The Finucane processes alone have already run into tens of millions. The new inquiry will add millions more. The wider legacy machine across Northern Ireland has cost hundreds of millions and is still spending. The scale is not serious. It is mad.

The Troubles lasted thirty years. The talk that came after has already run nearly as long. It is still going. We are now into yet another big inquiry into the killing of Pat Finucane. Lawyers’ grandchildren who were not even born when he died will still be drawing fees from it. Murder is wrong. No one serious argues otherwise. The real question is why some killings buy decades of process and others buy almost nothing.

Edgar Graham was shot in the head outside the law school at Queen’s on 7 December 1983. He was twenty-nine. A rising Ulster Unionist. A barrister. A lecturer. People who knew him said he had the makings of a leader. He was sharp. He was steady. He spoke plain and meant it. He was already a name in the Assembly. He had the kind of mind that cut through fog. He was not loud. He was clear. He could hold a room without raising his voice. Two men ran up behind him and fired at close range. He fell on the pavement outside the library. Students saw it. Staff saw it. The IRA said they did it. No one was ever found guilty. There has been no public inquiry. His name drifts up now and then and then sinks again.

I think about what was taken that morning. Not just a man. A possible future. Unionism has had leaders since. Few combined brains and backbone the way he did at that age. He was the sort who might have held a line when others wavered. He might have made the years that followed less wasted. He was young enough to have shaped a generation. Eoghan Harris once said the IRA blew out the brains of unionism that day. It still feels true. The shot removed someone who might have altered the shape of what came next. That loss is still there, even if most people have stopped saying his name. There is no centre named after him. No long list of funded reports. No permanent campaign. Just a short entry in the record and then quiet. The pavement where he fell has been walked over for more than forty years. People pass it every day. Most do not know.

Pat Finucane was shot fourteen times in front of his family in 1989. He was a solicitor. A large part of his work was with republican clients. Inquiry followed inquiry. Reports. Legal teams. Public money. In 2026 a full public inquiry with real powers of compulsion finally began. The machinery around that single death is now larger than entire truth processes run in other countries. It has already lasted decades. It will last more. The process has become a world of its own. Hearings. Lawyers. Statements. Fresh teams. New papers. Fresh funding. The meter keeps running. Each new stage brings more fees, more statements, more time. The work has no natural end.

Billy Fox was a Fine Gael Senator from Monaghan. A Protestant. A man who had spoken against the IRA. In March 1974 the IRA took him from a house near the border and shot him. The killing shocked people in the South at the time. It showed the IRA would murder southern politicians who stood in their way. Five men were later convicted. After that the story was allowed to fade. No long campaign. No sustained inquiry. Almost no public memory. A southern parliamentarian killed by the IRA for opposing them, and the years that followed treated it as a closed chapter. The shock of the time did not turn into lasting attention. The house near the border is still just a house.

Set the three deaths next to each other and the pattern is hard to miss.

One unionist lawyer is killed and the answer is near silence.
One solicitor whose practice sat close to the republican movement is killed and the answer becomes a decades-long industry.
One southern Senator who opposed the IRA is killed and the story is quietly filed away.

That is not accident. It is selection. The selection has consequences. It teaches people which deaths matter and which deaths can be left on the shelf. It shapes what is remembered and what is allowed to fade.

My father knew Pat Finucane. He wrote about him years later in plain terms.

“I first met Pat Finucane in 1980 at a high-level IRA finance meeting in Letterkenny… He came to visit me several times in Crumlin Road prison… Finucane wanted to represent me, but expressed no interest in my legal position. He was far more interested in what information I had given to the police… Pat Finucane was an effective agent for the IRA… Of course Finucane should not have been murdered… But he was not the blameless, innocent ‘human rights’ lawyer…”

That is the testimony of a man who was in the room. It has never been welcome. It remains on the record. The later portrait of Finucane has needed that kind of detail pushed to the margins. The cleaner the halo, the easier the campaign. Ruth Dudley Edwards has pointed out the insult to other victims when further millions are poured into one case while so many other killings receive almost no institutional attention. She is right. The money and the energy flow in one direction. They do not flow toward Edgar Graham. They do not flow toward Billy Fox. The imbalance is not hidden. It is simply not discussed. People learn which stories are safe to keep alive.

The same uneven standard applies south of the border.

In the 1970s parts of the Irish state looked the other way. Training camps operated in the hills and on the farms. Men came from the North and went back again. Weapons moved across the border with less trouble than they should have met. Safe houses stayed open for years. Some politicians gave cover or kept quiet. Some in the Garda stayed quiet too. Green fellow-travelling ran through sections of the southern establishment. It was never the whole state. It was enough. Those early years gave the IRA space to organise, to arm, to learn its trade. Knowledge spread. Methods improved. The organisation grew stronger because room had been left for it. People knew. Not everyone. Enough people knew. The looking-away was real.

Later governments shut much of that space down. The change was real. Some of the people involved came to regret what had been permitted. Regret does not raise the dead. The organisation that was given room did not stay inside the North. It carried its methods further. It took what it had learned and used it elsewhere. And when it decided to, it killed its critics in the South as well. Senator Fox remains the clearest single example. An Irish parliamentarian shot by the IRA for opposing them has never attracted the moral energy or the institutional machinery that surrounds selected northern cases. Edgar Graham has attracted even less. The quiet around both belongs to the same pattern. The pattern has lasted. It still lasts.

We are told again and again about the British Army, the UDR, the RUC. The stories are repeated, examined, re-examined. Files are opened. Statements are taken. New teams are formed. We hear far less about the help, the looking-away, the quiet green sympathy that existed in parts of the southern state during those first years. The lack of talk is treated as proof that little of weight happened. That is not true. The later change in southern policy was real. It does not cancel the earlier phase. The people who died stay dead. The widows and the children stay without the same long process. The silence is part of the selection.

There is another imbalance that shows itself when men who were there speak.

Many who served in the RUC and the UDR can still see the young republican as a human being who made terrible choices. They understand how a life can go wrong. That understanding makes the real psychopaths stand out more clearly. The reverse is less common. Many former IRA voices still speak of the RUC and the UDR as if the men in those uniforms had no individual faces.

Danny Morrison is not the usual republican voice. He reads. He thinks. When he speaks about Bloody Friday you can watch the tension. He first reaches for the old excuse — the British did not act fast enough on the warnings. Then he describes meeting RUC men who simply faced more bombs than they could handle that day. In that moment the eyes change. He knows. The weight is there, even if the full sentence is never spoken. Some men found the courage to say the whole truth and walk away from the machine. Most did not.

I have watched the legacy machine grow year after year. It feeds on certain deaths and ignores others. It pays well. It does not finish. Nuremberg finished. The South African commission finished. This one does not. New teams appear. New hearings are listed. Fresh funding is found. The meter keeps turning. The cost is not only money. It is the slow lesson that some lives deserve endless process and others deserve a short paragraph in the paper. It is the quiet signal that the past will be examined only along the lines that suit the best-organised voices. The machine has no reason to stop. Stopping would end the work. Ending the work would end the fees and the focus.

Edgar Graham’s killing bought no machine. There is no permanent centre. No rolling inquiry. No decades of paid analysis. The pavement where he fell is just a pavement. People walk over it and do not know. Billy Fox’s killing bought none. The house near the border is just a house. Pat Finucane’s killing bought an industry that is still running and still hiring. The difference is the point. The difference is what the process teaches.

The dreary steeples have now outlasted Nuremberg and the South African commission put together. They are no less dreary. The talk continues. The imbalance remains. And the dead who do not fit the preferred story stay silent.
